                                                  Abstract: The no-(k + 1)-in-line problem seeks the maximum number fk (n) of points
                                                  that can be selected from an n × n square lattice such that no k + 1 of them are collinear.
                                                  The problem was first posed more than 100 years ago for the special case k = 2 and has
                                                  remained open ever since. The general problem was recently resolved in the case k is not
                                                  small compared to n, as Kovács, Nagy and Szabó proved that the upper bound kn can be
                                                                                √
                                                  attained, provided that k > C n log nfor an absolute constant C. 
                                                      In this paper, we show that 1 − 2k kn ≤ fk (n) ≤ kn and 1 − 3k kn ≤ fk (n) ≤ kn hold for
                                                  every even k and odd k, respectively, provided that n is large enough. This is asymptotically
                                                  tight as k → ∞. Previously, only fk (n) = Ω(kn) was known due to Lefmann.
                                                      We present further improvements on the lower bounds for constant values of k when
                                                  k < 23 holds. All these bounds are based on randomised algebraic constructions.

                                           Key words and phrases: no-(k+1)-in-line problem, algebraic constructions, probabilistic method

Outline of the paper Our paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we set the main notation and the
general tools for the proofs. We also recall the main ideas and properties of the construction for k = 2 by
Hall et al. [13]. It relies on a construction over F p × F p with p ≤ n and the fact that for every n there
is a prime number p = pn ≤ n with pn /n → 1 (see [2]) which enables us to embed the construction to
[1, n] × [1, n] and get the same asymptotical result as for prime numbers. This theme will be applied in
our constructions as well: the size of our randomised constructions will be parametrized by an arbitrary
odd prime number, and this construction will be embedded into the grid [1, n] × [1, n].
     Then in Section 3, we use this construction as a building block to prove Theorem 1.5. The more
technical part of the argument, consisting mainly of the required computations, is carried out in the
next section. The general lower bound of Theorem 1.5 can be slightly improved for sporadic cases of k
via the combination of two further building blocks, relying on the construction for k = 2 by Hall et al.,
and Appendix A is dedicated to the refinement of the techniques detailed in Section 3 in order to get
improved bounds on fk (n) when k is a small constant. Using a slightly different technique, we improve
the lower bound in the case k = 3 and sketch the proof of Theorem 1.6 in Section 5 to complete the table
of Theorem 1.7. The exact calculations are carried out in Appendix B. Finally, we discuss some open and
related problems in Section 6.

2.1   Grids and secants
Let us recall the following definitions and notations from the previous paper by the authors [14]. The
notation [a, b] refers to the integer interval {x ∈ Z : a ≤ x ≤ b}. A grid is a set G = I1 ×I2 where I1 = [a1 , b1 ]
and I2 = [a2 , b2 ] are integer intervals. We say that G is an n1 × n2 grid, where n j = |I j | = b j − a j + 1 for
 j = 1, 2. We call G a square grid if n1 = n2 .
    A secant ℓ of a grid G is a line in the Euclidean plane that meets G in at least two points. We call a line
ℓ generic if it is not horizontal or vertical. Note that each generic secant ℓ has a unique direction vector
v = (vx , vy ) such that vx ∈ Z+ , vy ∈ Z \ {0} and gcd(vx , vy ) = 1. The modulus of ℓ is M = max(|vx |, |vy |).
    Given an integer k ≥ 2, we call S ⊆ G a no-(k + 1)-in-line set if |S ∩ ℓ| ≤ k for every Euclidean line ℓ
(or equivalently, for every secant ℓ of G).

2.2   The no-three-in-line construction of Hall, Jackson, Sudbery and Wild
In this subsection, we recall the main construction of Hall, Jackson, Sudbery and Wild in [13], which
gives a no-three-in-line set of size 3(p − 1) in a 2p × 2p grid for any prime p ≥ 3. We start by recalling
their most important definitions and lemmas, as our new constructions are also based on these.

Definition 2.1 (Congruent points in Z × Z). Two points (x1 , y1 ) and (x2 , y2 ) are called congruent mod p,
or simply congruent, if x1 ≡ x2 (mod p) and y1 ≡ y2 (mod p). A class is the set of all points in Z × Z
congruent to a certain point. Note that classes naturally correspond to elements of F p × F p . The base
point of a class is its unique element in [0, p − 1] × [0, p − 1].

                                H(c, p) := {(x, y) ∈ Z × Z : xy ≡ c (mod p)}.

6     Concluding remarks
The main results of our paper establish a lower bound on fk (n) by | fk (n) − kn| = O(n) for any value
of k. Yet, we believe that this holds in a stronger form, namely | fk (n) − kn| = o(n) also holds for
k := k(n) ≫ 1. Notice however that Theorem 1.6 gives evidence that we cannot expect probabilistic
bounds using multiple Hall–Jackson–Sudbery–Wild type 2p × 2p constructions to be near-optimal. As
a matter of fact, we conjecture that even the Theorem 2.7 of Hall et al. might not be asymptotically
tight. On the other hand, as we mentioned at the end of the introduction, Grebennikov and Kwan proved
that | fk (n) − kn| = o(n) holds for k := k(n) ≫ 1 in a strong form: fk (n) − kn = 0 for k := k(n) ≥ 1037
provided that n ≥ k.
     Similarly to the case when k was not small, discussed in [14], the no-(k + 1)-in-line property of the
presented constructions essentially relied on the fact that they intersect the lines with small modulus,
i.e., lines of slope ±1 beside the horizontal and vertical lines, in at most k points. Several well-known
problems have similar requirements, see e.g. [6, 7]. The most prominent example might be the so-called
n-queens problem. To solve it, one aims to place n chess queens on an n × n chessboard so that no
two queens share the same row, column, or diagonal. The number of such placements was determined
asymptotically just recently by Simkin [19], see also the preceding work of Bowtell, Keevash [4] and
Luria, Simkin [16].


A     Improvement for certain small values of k
A.1    The main idea
Let us recall Theorem 3.2: we started from S(W ) ⊆ G and removed a point set R from it to obtain a
no-(k + 1)-in-line construction P(W ). In this section, along the idea of Theorem 3.4, we alter the process
of finding R from Section 3 to obtain stronger lower bounds for fk (n) when k is small, at least for certain
values of k.
     For example, in the case W = {c} and k = 2, Subsection 2.2 essentially details an optimal choice
for the points R to be removed to gain the largest no-3-in-line set S2 (c, p) inside S(W ). This value of
|Pk (W )| is a sharpening of the estimate of (3.1) that we used in Section 3. Similarly, for W = {c} and
k = 3, removing the points described in Subsection 2.3 gives the largest no-4-in-line set inside S(W ),
namely S3 (c, p).
     We mix these optimal removals with the probabilistic process of Section 3 to obtain the following
process. For arbitrary W ∈ Ω p and k ∈ N, we remove points from the disjoint union S0 := S(W ) =
  c∈W S4 (c, p) ⊂ G in two steps to obtain a no-(k + 1)-in-line set.

B     Full proof of Theorem 1.6 – the detailed case of k = 3
In this section, we prove Theorem 1.6. The idea is similar to the HJSW construction, but this time we use
a grid of size 3p × 3p (where p ≥ 3 is a prime), hence consisting of 6 × 6 blocks of normal points. Again
we will take the points of the modular hyperbola H(c, p) that lie in certain areas of the grid.
    Recall that a point (x, y) ∈ Z × Z is normal if p ∤ x and p ∤ y.

B.1    Congruence class pairing for modulus 2 lines
Recall from Theorem 2.5 that if a line of slope +1 met H(c, p) in two congruence classes, then these
classes were (x, y) and (−y, −x) for some x, y ∈ F∗p , and for lines of slope −1 we had (x, y) and (y, x).
Now we reiterate some observations for modulus 2 lines, that is, lines of slope ±2 or ± 21 from Section 5.
Observation B.1. Let ℓ be a line of modulus 2. If ℓ meets H(c, p) in two congruence classes, with one
of them being (x, y) for some x, y ∈ F∗p , then depending on whether ℓ has slope +2, −2, + 12 , or − 21 , the
other congruence class must be − 2y , −2x , 2y , 2x , −2y, − 2x , or 2y, 2x respectively.

    When ℓ meets H(c, p) in just one congruence class, the two mentioned congruence classes must
coincide (e.g., we have y = −2x in the slope +2 case).
